The shape of it

Three documents arrive on a schedule and one screen is live.

The morning brief is the plan for the operating day. The mid-day brief is the day's second issue; it forecasts its own 24 hours and its plan takes over at 15:00. The weekly report is the record of how the plans did. The dashboard shows the same day as the brief, with the market drawn against it as the day runs.

All of it is advisory. Nothing here operates equipment, and every recommendation is yours to take or leave.

Dispatch briefs

Two per operating day: the morning brief at 06:00 CT and the mid-day brief at 13:45 CT. Each lists the charge and discharge windows we'd run and what each one is worth. Every brief you've been issued stays on the shelf and can be searched by date. An old brief keeps the figures it was issued with, so if your asset's rating has changed since, the older document still shows the older rating. That's what its plan was built on.

The mid-day brief

It appears under its own day, beside that morning's brief. It's issued at 13:45 CT with the next day's day-ahead prices in hand, forecasts its own 24 hours, and from 15:00 its plan is the one in force. The brief lists what it changed from the morning's, block by block. On a day without one, the morning plan stands for the whole day. Which plan governs which hours is covered in Two issues a day.

The dashboard

The live screen for today, and the one to keep open while the day runs. It carries the same plan as the morning brief, with conditions drawn against it as they change. Past days are kept, so you can open the screen as it stood on any day you want to look at again.

Reports

The weekly record: what the plans were, what happened, and what the difference was worth against your baseline. Red weeks are shown as red weeks. The fee line sits inside the report, beside the gains it's a share of, rather than on a separate billing screen. Reports are entitled separately from the daily documents, so they open for some seats on an account and not others. If Reports isn't in your menu, yours is one of the others.

The Quarterly Report

One per quarter, on the Reports shelf with its own tag. It closes the quarter: recorded, gain over baseline, and the Neurevo fee come first, then the invoice line, then the fee worked out week by week and added up, what carried in and out, and where every figure came from. It issues once the quarter's last day has settled. That's about ten days after the quarter ends when your settlement data reaches us through ERCOT's report stream, and about sixty when it comes from ERCOT's public disclosure. It is final on issue. How it closes is in The quarter close.

Messages

Notices from us to this account: scheduled maintenance, a change to a rule your fee or your documents are worked out under, and anything else the account should hear from us in writing. Messages stay there, dated, newest first, and can be found by date, kind, or the words in them. We don't take them down; one that needs correcting is answered by a new one. More in Messages.

Welcome and Account

The welcome packet is the day-one document and stays where you can re-read it. It's the first entry in Messages, with its revision history under it; the Reports shelf holds reports only. Account holds your seat, your entity, your asset, the instrument in force, and the people on the account. You can add and remove people there and decide whether they see Reports.

How the plan is built

Every dispatch brief carries one plan: a short list of charge and discharge blocks, each with an hour window, an energy figure, and a planned price. This chapter explains what those figures mean and how the list is arrived at.

What a block is

Each row of The plan is one instruction: a verb, a window, and how much energy moves in it.

Windows align to the hour because the day-ahead market clears hourly. There's no half-hour in the plan because there's no half-hour in the prices it's built on.

The three energy figures, and why they differ

Three numbers on the page look like they should match and don't. They're measured at different places.

  • Charge is what you draw from the grid.
  • State of charge is what's stored in the battery.
  • Discharge is what you deliver to the grid.

A charge of 228 MWh doesn't put 228 MWh in the battery, and 212 MWh stored doesn't come back out as 212 MWh. The losses sit between each pair.

Round-trip losses are already inside the megawatt-hours printed on the plan, so don't apply them again. The wear allowance ($3 per MWh discharged, the figure on your order form) is deducted on top.

Where the prices come from

A charge block's planned price sits under the discharge blocks that follow it. The spread between them is what the cycle is worth.

The per-hour prices under each block are the planned prices for those hours; the figure beside the block is their average. These are what the plan was built on. They aren't a settlement, and settled prices decide the outcome.

What the plan starts from

Each issue solves from the state of charge the prior issue leaves at its first hour. On the morning brief that handover is usually empty, because the plan it takes over from usually ends empty. But the plan is solved from the handover itself, not from an assumption. A morning that inherits a charged battery is solved from what it inherits.

The mid-day plan is solved from the state of charge the morning plan hands over at 15:00, under what's left of the same cycling allowance (see Two issues a day). That figure is printed on the mid-day brief, beside the plan.

Either way the plan is solved at planned prices, up to 1.5 cycles a day.

The cycling allowance, and which day it belongs to

The allowance is 1.5 cycles a day: 1.5 times your battery's energy rating, in megawatt-hours discharged.

It's counted over the morning issue's own 24 hours, 07:00 CT to 07:00 CT the next morning, not the calendar date. A block at 03:00 or 06:00 tomorrow sits inside the window that opened with this morning's brief and counts against this morning's allowance.

The mid-day plan draws on what's left of that same allowance for the whole of its own 24 hours, including the 07:00–15:00 stretch tomorrow that reaches past the morning's day. The mid-day brief prints the subtraction: the full allowance less what the morning half has already discharged. When tomorrow's morning issue takes over at 07:00, it opens a new day with a full allowance of its own, and nothing the mid-day plan discharged before then counts against it.

When the plan is replaced

Which plan is in force at any hour, how the 13:45 issue takes over at 15:00, and what one issue hands to the next: all in Two issues a day.

Two issues a day

You get two dispatch briefs a day, and each is archived at its own issue time. Each forecasts its own 24 hours and plans them. This chapter covers what each one covers, which plan is in force when, and how the handover works.

The two issues

IssuedIts planBeyond its plan
Morning brief06:00 CT07:00 today to 07:00 tomorrowa provisional schedule
Mid-day brief13:45 CT15:00 today to 15:00 tomorrowa provisional schedule

The 13:45 issue forecasts its own 24 hours the same way the morning issue does: its own price range, its own spike probabilities and calls, and its own plan. Its plan is priced on the next day's day-ahead prices, which have usually posted by 13:45. Its plan window, 15:00 today to 15:00 tomorrow, is also the window its chart draws and the window its record is graded on. All three end where the plan ends.

The newest issue governs the hours both cover

The two plans overlap from 15:00 today to 07:00 tomorrow. The newer issue wins where they overlap:

  • until 15:00 today, the 06:00 issue's plan;
  • from 15:00 today, the 13:45 issue's plan;
  • from 07:00 tomorrow, the next morning's 06:00 issue's plan. If that issue doesn't arrive, the 13:45 plan carries on to 15:00 tomorrow.

This is a rule about which plan is in force. It isn't a judgment between the two issues, and no brief compares them.

Both issues count toward what's billed. An issue filed more than an hour after its time, or not filed at all, takes the whole day out of your fee, not just the hours it plans (see Baseline, Measured Gain, and the fee).

The 13:00 and 14:00 hours are never re-planned at 13:45. The 13:00 hour is already under way and the 14:00 hour has less than an hour's notice. A block the morning plan has running at 13:45 runs to 15:00 on the morning plan. From 15:00 the 13:45 plan either continues it or withdraws the rest, and the mid-day brief says which.

What this issue changes

Directly under its plan, the mid-day brief lists every block either issue schedules from 15:00 to 07:00: which ones the 13:45 issue kept, moved, shortened, extended, or re-sized; which it withdrew; and which it added. Each comes with its hours and its energy. For example: Discharge moves from Thu 18:00–20:00 to Thu 19:00–21:00 · 196 MWh, or Charge Fri 08:00–10:00 (provisional in the 06:00 issue) withdrawn · 125 MWh. The hours from 07:00 to 15:00 tomorrow are listed against the morning brief's provisional blocks for those hours. Nothing in the next hour ever moves; the first hour the 13:45 issue plans is 15:00.

The morning brief carries the mirror sentence: This plan governs from 07:00; the 13:45 issue re-forecasts and re-plans from 15:00, and its brief lists what it changed. It also lists what it changed from the previous day's 13:45 issue between 07:00 and 15:00.

An instruction you're still holding is never dropped quietly. A block the newer issue doesn't keep is printed as withdrawn, with its hours and its energy.

The handover

At 15:00 the morning plan hands over two things: the state of charge it leaves in the battery, and what's left of the day's cycling allowance after the morning half's discharges. The 13:45 plan is solved from both.

That's why the allowance figure on the mid-day brief is smaller than the full daily one on a day with morning discharges, and equal to it on a day without. It's one budget over the whole day. Which day it's counted over is in How the plan is built.

Each issue is filed before its first instruction. The two clocks every brief carries, issued and filed (your shelf row shows the date stamp only), are defined in Provenance and verification.

When the next day's prices are late

Occasionally the next day's day-ahead prices haven't posted by 13:45. The mid-day brief says so when it happens and plans those hours on our forecast instead.

The overnight round trip

A plan that ends its window by charging and then discharging again commits you to a trade in the hours the forecast knows least about.

From the 06:00 issue of 28 Sep 2026, that overnight round trip is planned like any other block. It's scheduled whenever it's the plan's best use of the battery over that window, by the same test as every other hour. Because each issue plans against the information it has, the two issues of one day can reach different answers about the same overnight trade; the 13:45 issue has the next day's day-ahead prices and the 06:00 issue didn't. Where they differ, the newer issue governs the overlapping hours, and the mid-day brief's What this issue changes list spells it out block by block.

Plans issued through the 13:45 issue of 27 Sep 2026 held that trade to an extra test, round-trip breakeven, and said under the plan when the closing round trip was declined. Those briefs are kept as issued. The date of the change is in What changed.

Mid-day briefs issued before this rule

Mid-day briefs issued before the 13:45 issue began forecasting its own hours re-planned the morning's hours from 15:00 on the morning's own forecast. They said so on the page and are kept as issued. The date the rule changed is in What changed.

Provisional blocks and the forward range

Under Beyond today, each brief carries two things that aren't part of the committed plan: a price band and a list of provisional blocks. Neither is graded.

The forward range

Past the graded window, the band shows a range of typical outcomes for those hours rather than a forecast for that particular day. The committed window above it is the day's forecast.

Higher prices are still possible out there. We don't grade ourselves on it.

Provisional blocks

The committed plan is the issue's own plan window: 07:00 to 07:00 for a morning brief, 15:00 to 15:00 for a mid-day brief. The provisional blocks extend it to the end of that issue's forecast, so a day the system can't be attended to (a holiday, a weekend, an outage) still has a rough schedule to work from.

Every new issue re-solves everything ahead of it, so expect these blocks to move. The further out the forecast reaches, the more it degrades against settled prices.

On the page, provisional blocks are drawn in line shade and labelled Provisional · re-solved at the next issue · not graded. Committed blocks are solid.

A block that begins inside the current issue's own plan window is drawn solid and listed as committed, whichever solve first scheduled those hours.

Why a range and not a price

These hours have no single planned price behind them, so the brief prints a band rather than a point. A single number for an hour the forecast doesn't cover would look like a commitment nobody made.

Spike forecasts and probabilities

Each brief carries one operator line about spikes, with a table of hourly probabilities under it. This chapter explains how to read both.

The one line

$200+ spike · peak near cutoff · not forecast (2.9%, trigger 4.8%)

The first figure is the highest probability any hour in the window carries for a price above $200. The second is the level that probability has to reach before the brief states a spike forecast for the day.

The trigger is fixed ahead of the day and doesn't move during it, so the same probability always gives the same answer.

The state words

Between the threshold and the figures, the line carries a short phrase saying where the day's peak probability sits against the trigger. There are five:

  • peak above cutoff: the peak is at or above the trigger. This is the only state in which the brief states a spike forecast for the day.
  • peak near cutoff: below the trigger, but at least half of it.
  • peak below cutoff: under half the trigger.
  • peak at the floor: the peak rests on the registered 1.0% probability floor, so the forecast gives the threshold no support at all.
  • cutoff at the floor: the trigger itself rests on that floor, which leaves nothing usable to measure a peak against.

Only the first carries a forecast. The next three all read "not forecast" and differ only in how much of the distance to the trigger the day covered. The half-way mark between near and below is a reading convention for this phrase; no threshold, grade, or figure on the brief moves with it.

$200 is the only threshold with a stated spike forecast. The others carry none, for two different reasons:

  • $500, $1,000 and $2,000 rest permanently on the 1.0% probability floor, so a spike forecast against them would fire every day and mean nothing.
  • $100 carries an hourly probability but no day-level spike forecast, because no trigger has been set for it. One set later would apply only to days graded after that.

The probability table

Each cell is the probability that the hourly real-time price exceeds that threshold within that window.

Daggered cells sit at the registered 1.0% probability floor. Read them as "nothing in the forecast argues for this threshold," not as a measured one-in-a-hundred. On the brief the legend is the short form: † = 1% floor.

The graded record on the brief

The brief prints the $200+ record over the last 30 graded days, and what has moved since the last issue.

A day is graded the morning after it settles, so the newest graded day on a brief is always a day or two behind the issue date. The figures are our own graded record against settled ERCOT prices, misses included. A day we forecast that didn't arrive stays in the count. So does a day that arrived with no forecast.

What a "hit" and a "miss" mean

The record on a brief grades the morning issue's call. A day counts as forecast when the morning brief stated a $200+ spike forecast for it. Each graded day is then one of four things:

  • hit: a forecast day on which an hour settled at $200 or above;
  • not arrived: a forecast day on which no hour did. It stays in the forecast count and is never a hit;
  • miss: a day with no forecast on which an hour settled at $200 or above. The record names each one by date;
  • quiet: a day with no forecast and no such hour. Most days.

The 13:45 issue's calls are graded on a separate record of their own. No brief pools the two or sets one against the other.

Provenance and verification

Every brief ends with a short line saying where it came from. This chapter explains each part of it.

What the briefs are built from

Public ERCOT market and system data, and public weather data. Nothing on a brief comes from anything you haven't seen or couldn't get yourself.

Archived at issue

A brief is rendered from the forecast archived at its issue time and is not re-forecast. The record behind it is fixed when the brief is issued. Open the same brief a month later and its figures say what they said that morning. Its design may have been re-issued since, and the brief as first sent is always on file as revision 0; see What changed.

Each brief carries two clocks, and since 10 October 2026 your shelf shows neither. A shelf row names the kind of brief and the date stamp only. Issued is the time on the brief's face, the instant its forecast and plan are stated from (06:00 or 13:45 CT), and the document's own footer carries it. Filed is when the finished document reached your shelf. If a brief has been corrected or re-issued, the row's Changes to this report gives the correction's date and what changed.

The digest printed on the brief, a short SHA-256 fingerprint, identifies that archived record. The full digest is on the verification surface in your portal, where it can be checked against the record itself.

A mid-day brief carries two digests: its own, and the digest of the 06:00 issue it supersedes from 15:00. Since the 13:45 issue forecasts its own 24 hours, every forecast figure on a mid-day brief comes from the record behind its own digest. The second digest names the morning record whose blocks its What this issue changes list is read against. A mid-day brief issued before that rule drew the morning's forecast and named the morning's digest as the one it followed; it's kept as issued.

Graded the next day

Each issue is graded against settled ERCOT prices once its own 24 hours have settled. That's why the record on a brief always runs to a day or two before the issue date.

Information vintage

The forecast is limited by its oldest input, not its newest. Where a brief's inputs aren't all current to the same hour, the older one is the bound.

Where the dollars are stated

Every price on a brief is our forecast for your own hub, the hub named on your order form. Every live and settled price on your dashboard and every dollar on your weekly report is at that same hub. Where a page was built on another hub's prices, it says so on page one. Your resource settles at its own node rather than at the hub; the difference between the two is the node's local component, and it's outside every dollar we report.

How far ahead a brief claims

A brief carries a 48-hour forecast. It states no seven-day posture, and nothing beyond the forecast's reach is inferred in its place.

Reading the charts

Each brief carries two charts. Both are drawn against the clock hours on the axis, and both show the same plan the table above them lists.

The next 24 hours

The header names the two layers:

  • on a morning brief, Forecast price · the plan;
  • on a mid-day brief, 13:45 forecast · 24-hour plan, because the price line is the 13:45 issue's own forecast and the blocks are its own plan.

Each brief draws its own forecast across its own plan window (the two windows are in Two issues a day). The hour before the window opens sits at the chart's left edge, 06:00 on a morning brief and 14:00 on a mid-day brief, drawn but not planned, because that's the hour the earlier plan still holds. A mid-day brief issued before the 13:45 issue began forecasting its own hours drew the morning's forecast, and its header said so. Those briefs are kept as issued.

The shaded columns are the plan's own blocks, charge windows in one tone and discharge windows in another. There's one column for every row in The plan and one row for every column. The chart and the table are the same list.

Beyond today

The second chart runs past the graded window. The band on it is a range of typical outcomes for those hours, and the blocks drawn in line shade are provisional. See Provisional blocks and the forward range.

On a phone

Both charts redraw at a narrower frame rather than scrolling sideways. The axis labels thin out, but nothing is cut off the edge.

The live dispatch dashboard

The dashboard answers three questions, in order: what should I be doing right now, when is the next action, and how is the market tracking against the plan. The instruction card, the countdown, and the plan chart answer them. The figures are on the screen; what each one means is here.

What the screen shows

The instruction card names what the plan of record asks for in the current hour (CHARGE, DISCHARGE, or HOLD) with the block's energy and its window. It's advisory, never an instruction to operate equipment, and the card says so on its face.

The countdown is the same block's clock: how long until it starts, or how long the one running has left.

The schedule sits under the card. Each day, Today and Tomorrow, gets a strip across its own 24 hours that shows where the blocks fall, and under it one row per block in clock order, each tagged PASSED, NOW, AHEAD, or PROVISIONAL. A block a newer issue withdrew stays in its place on the schedule, struck through and tagged WITHDRAWN, with the issue that withdrew it named on its row.

PASSED means the window has closed. It doesn't mean the block was executed. This screen reads nothing from your plant and can't know whether it was.

PROVISIONAL means the block comes from the forward solve past the end of the newest issue's plan window. It's re-solved at the next issue and never graded.

The hour strip is one row that sets the forecast against what has settled. It names the hour and your hub, then shows our forecast price for that hour, then the newest settled fifteen-minute price at your hub inside that same hour, labelled with the time it's settled through. The last cell is the difference, the settled price less the forecast. It states the difference with its direction, for or against the plan, keyed to whether the hour is inside a charge window or a discharge one. A price below the forecast is good news while you're buying and bad news while you're selling, and the strip says which. Until a fifteen-minute interval of the hour has settled, the settled cell reads pending and the difference is left out. The strip never shows an earlier hour's price in its place.

Every price on this screen is at your hub, the hub named on your order form, with one exception, labelled where it's drawn. The Prints' five-minute row and the live market monitor both watch ERCOT's system-wide price, which isn't a price at your hub. On a congested day the two can sit on opposite sides of zero.

THE PLAN draws two price lines and the plan's own blocks. Our forecast runs the width of the frame and is drawn broken past the end of the committed window. The settled price at your hub is drawn interval by interval as each fifteen minutes publishes, with a dot on the newest one. When ERCOT's live system demand is drawn as well, it's the dotted line against the right-hand axis, in gigawatts: market-wide, and not a price. Each past day's pale line is the price that morning's plan was solved against, and the bars beneath it are the blocks that plan scheduled.

THE PRINTS is a tape on its own clock, with a ledger locked to it column for column. Its System-wide row is ERCOT's system-wide five-minute price, the system lambda the market publishes about five to ten minutes after each SCED run. Nothing settles at it and it carries no congestion. Its Settled row is the fifteen-minute settled price at your hub, printed once its interval has closed, which is why the current hour is blank there while the row above it moves every five minutes. The two rows are two quantities at two cadences, and the gap between them is real; we don't manufacture a five-minute settlement out of the system-wide print. Zoomed out, the tape rolls the five-minute prints into fifteen- and sixty-minute buckets, each a time-weighted average that its row header names. An averaged bucket counts as settled only once every fifteen-minute interval inside it has settled. Until then it reads pending.

Click either graph and a readout of the figures at that moment opens and stays. It's a layer you can select text from, and it closes on a click anywhere outside it or on Escape.

How the screen is kept current

The badge beside the As-of time reads updated 21:04 · next 21:14. Those two clocks are the whole refresh discipline.

The screen is rebuilt on a fixed cadence by a scheduled job on our machine, and the time in the masthead is when it was last built. The plan it draws is the one archived at its own issue. Between issues nothing re-solves it; what moves is the market around it.

If more than thirty minutes have passed on your own clock since that moment, the job has stopped and nothing on the page describes the market since. The badge turns amber when that happens, measured against your clock, since the page can't know the time on its own.

When a newer build lands, the page loads it by itself. If the browser can't reach the file's source (opening it straight off a disk, say), the page simply ages and the warning still fires.

A page pinned to one moment rather than rebuilt says frozen in the same badge and doesn't refresh.

The two boundaries on the plan chart

The upright is now. The rule to its right is where the plan stops being committed: the end of the newest issue's own plan window, 15:00 tomorrow after a 13:45 issue, or 07:00 tomorrow on a day whose newest issue is the morning's. The masthead's Committed from line names the hour that window opens. Everything right of that rule is provisional; the hatched bars beyond it are those blocks, and the forecast line is drawn broken from the same instant. A 13:45 block from 07:00 tomorrow is drawn solid and tagged Fallback · superseded by the 06:00 issue from 07:00. It stands only if that issue doesn't arrive.

What the stores actually hold

The measured legs stop at the last real print. Empty space on either graph is empty record, never an estimate, and no price is drawn across a gap.

The settled leg at your hub and The Prints' system-wide row each run only as far as its store holds inside the drawn span. A break inside either one is drawn as a gap and never bridged. A day on which no issue record was archived draws no plan at all; the empty column is the statement.

Nothing is drawn past the record's reach. The shaded band at the right-hand end is empty on purpose, and it's labelled.

Where our own settlement store is behind the market, the SETTLED row says so beside its own header, counted off the cells you're looking at.

The plan of record, and the day's second issue

The plan on the screen is the plan of record: the newest issue's plan over its own hours. Plans are issued on a declared schedule, the morning brief at 06:00 CT and the mid-day brief at 13:45 CT, and each forecasts and plans its own 24 hours. The morning issue governs the day until the 13:45 issue lands; from 15:00 that issue governs the hours it covers. A block the morning issue scheduled and the 13:45 issue withdrew is struck through on the schedule. A past day is redrawn this way only where its 13:45 issue is on file in the current form; a day from the earlier mid-day brief keeps the morning issue's bars for the whole day. On a day with no 13:45 issue, the morning plan stands for the whole day. Nothing else on this screen changes the plan, and the plan doesn't react to the market as the day moves.

The hour prices beside each block are the prices the plan was solved against, archived at its issue. They're what the plan expected, not what the market has done, and they aren't a recommendation about which hours to take.

Every figure on the screen comes from public market data and from the forecast archived and hashed at its own issue time, the same record the daily brief is rendered from. Nothing on the screen is hand-authored.

The spike forecast

The screen states a spike forecast against $200+ and no other threshold. The cutoff beside it is fixed ahead of the day and doesn't move with the market. There is no live recalibration.

The cutoffs for $500+, $1,000+ and $2,000+ rest on the 1.0% probability floor, so a spike forecast against them would fire every day and mean nothing. The screen collapses them to one line naming the floor.

The live market monitor is a monitor condition, not an instruction. Neurevo advises the plan it issued and doesn't recommend discharging into an excursion or recharging for a second one. Those calls need hindsight, the downside is real, and they're yours. The screen's job is to make sure you aren't the last to know.

What the screen does not do

It doesn't re-plan between issues. The market is drawn against the plan, and the plan holds until the next issue.

It reads nothing from your plant: no state of charge, no breaker state, no inverter output, no connection of any kind. How much of a window can actually be taken is yours to know. The plan asks for the whole of each window as far as the battery's state allows, and the volume beside each block is what the plan asked for, not what's available.

It operates nothing. Nothing on the page does anything to the battery.

It doesn't advise the speculative play. Discharging into an excursion or recharging for a second one are hindsight calls, and they stay with you.

Messages

Messages is where we write to this account. For the seats that see Reports, the welcome packet sits at the top, with its revision history under it; the Reports shelf holds reports only. Under it is every notice we've posted to the account, newest first, each dated the day it was posted.

What appears there

Each message is one of three kinds, and its tag says which:

  • Rule: a change to a rule your fee or your documents are worked out under, in plain words, with the date it applies from.
  • Maintenance: a scheduled period when the portal will be down for an update, with its start and end in Central time.
  • Notice: anything else the account should hear from us in writing, such as a re-issue of your documents and the reason for it.

Messages are kept for the life of the account. We don't take them down or edit them; a message that needs correcting is answered by a new one that says so. The date boxes, the kind list, and the search box above the list find any message by when it was posted, what kind it is, or the words in it.

Scheduled maintenance

A maintenance notice is posted before the window it announces. During that window your briefs come to you by email instead of through the portal, so each plan of record still reaches you on time while the portal is down.

The weekly report — how to read it

When your weekly arrives

Each weekly report is issued once, when every day of its week has settled on your settlement route, and it's never re-issued for settlement. What it shows is what the week is billed on. That's about ten days after the week ends when your settlement data reaches us through ERCOT's report stream, and about sixty when it comes from ERCOT's public record.

The lines under the report's masthead say when it was issued, that it's final, and where its settlement data came from: for example Issued Thu 8 Oct 2026 and Final · settlement stream, or Final · ERCOT public record on the sixty-day route.

A final weekly is restated only when a rule we apply to every client changes, never because a figure resettled after it was issued (the Quarterly Report's true-up carries that). A restated weekly keeps its original issue line, says when it was re-issued, and carries a dated correction line under its masthead naming the rule and the figures that moved.

A week's report appears on your Reports shelf when the week has settled, and nothing of it is shown before then. The shelf says when the next weekly arrives and which week it covers.

Reading the day table

What the character bands mean

Day character is the band the day's highest settled dispatch-window hour reached. The bands are $100–$199, $200–$299, $300–$499, $500–$999, and $1,000+. QUIET is a day whose peak stayed below $100. A day we hold no settled price for carries no band.

What counts as a spike

A spike is a run of consecutive settled dispatch-window hours at $100 or more. Any hour below that ends it, so $120, $95, $130 is two spikes, and one day can hold more than one. Each spike is banded by its own highest hour.

The blocks at the top of the report count spikes; the day table counts days, banded by the day's highest hour. A day that held a second spike carries it on its own line with that spike's own peak, so both counts can be read off the page.

Caught and missed

A spike is CAUGHT when the plan of record for that day discharges in at least one hour inside it, and MISSED when it doesn't. A spike we hold no plan schedule for isn't judged either way; "we didn't measure" and "nothing was taken" are different statements, and only one of them is a miss. On a week whose plans were re-issued, and so carry no graded forecast, the rule is the same (the plan's own hours decide it) and the spike line says so: plan: discharged in the spike (no forecast graded).

Days your battery carried ancillary service

Where your settlement record shows capacity committed to ancillary services in an hour, the day table prints what was in reserve and what was traded at the hour of the largest commitment, and the note says which hours. Every figure on the page, and the fee, is measured on the capacity you traded; a day with the whole battery in reserve is paused: no fee, and no credit against your baseline. What the commitment earned is yours; it is not in these figures.

The RESERVE / TRADED column prints only in a week that carried ancillary service. On a paused day the plan captured and its share of the ceiling still print, because they grade the plan against the market, and the spike cell prints the spike itself, without the plan and you lines. A spike inside hours with the whole battery in reserve reads you: set to ancillary service. The week total counts the week's hours by state: the whole battery in reserve, divided between reserve and trade, and fully traded.

What "captured" means inside a spike line

Inside a spike line, captured is the money that leg took out of that excursion: the settled price of each hour multiplied by what it discharged in that hour, less the wear allowance on the same volume.

It's gross of the cost of charging, because the charge that filled the battery happened in other hours and was spent before the excursion arrived. The day's own PLAN CAPTURED and PLAN CAPTURE, % OF CEILING columns are the whole day, charging cost included, and they're the figures the rest of the report is built on. The weekly says this beside the table in one line: Spike-line figures are gross of charging; day columns are net.

Plan captured, capture, and the ceiling

Plan captured is the archived plan scored against that day's settled prices, before the ancillary envelope. It's the plan's total for the day, not the gain over your baseline. On a day that carried ancillary service and isn't paused, it's measured on the capacity you traded, as line (C) of the fee ledger is, and the day's note says which hours. The week total row, the figures at the top of the report, and the week-by-week table are measured on the capacity traded (from 10 October 2026), and the note under the day table says so.

Plan capture, % of ceiling, beside it, is the plan captured as a share of what perfect foresight on the same prices would have taken: how close the plan came to the peak. Both are simulated on your own asset, from the plan of record as first issued and never re-run. Every day we graded appears in the table, including the mediocre ones. A report itself can be re-issued as a correction, with the original kept on file as revision 0; see What changed for what a re-issue does and doesn't touch.

Baseline is what your own baseline schedule would have earned that day on the same settled prices: the day's part of the BASELINE on the week-by-week table, and the floor your gain is measured from.

Recorded & plan deviation is what your own settled dispatch earned that day, on the same settled prices and net of the same wear allowance: the day's part of Recorded on the first page. Under it, in the same cell, is that figure as a percentage of the plan captured. 100% is the goal. A percentage at or above 90% prints in green; below 90% prints in red, marked ▼ so it reads the same on a black-and-white print. No fee is shown on any day; the fee is worked out week by week and appears on the week-by-week table. A day that isn't billed still shows its baseline and what you captured, because the dispatch happened; only the fee leaves it out. Each day shows the whole day, billed or not. The week-by-week table carries only the billed days.

The Week total row at the foot of the table adds up the plan captured, the baseline, and your capture, and gives your capture as a percentage of the week's plan in the same green and red. A paused day adds nothing to the total: its own line still prints the plan captured before the ancillary envelope, but the total's plan, like its baseline and recorded figure, is measured on the capacity traded, so it is the same figure the first page prints under "What the plan was worth", and the percentage is taken over it. Its plan capture, % of ceiling is the week's plan as a share of the week's ceiling on the same capacity, the same percentage the first page prints beneath that figure. When every day of the week is billed and none is paused, its three sums match the week-by-week table's THE PLAN, BASELINE, and RECORDED for the week. A day not billed is in the total but not in the week-by-week row, and the note under that table names it.

When an issue was filed late or not filed, the day's note says so, says the day wasn't billed, names the issue and when it was filed, and adds the day's gain over your baseline, which is yours (or says nothing was owed that day).

The ceiling is the perfect-foresight figure: what a schedule built with complete knowledge of the settled prices would have earned on your battery. It's a mark to measure against, not a target. It's printed in dollars on the week summary because it's your own number, computed on your own plant and your own settled prices.

The fee ledger measures the same plan after the ancillary capacity envelope and prints both figures, so the two can be reconciled.

Why a day gets flagged, and how a flag closes

A day is flagged when the plan captured less than 50% of that day's own ceiling. Below 50% is flagged; at or above is not; there are no exceptions. A micro-opportunity day below the line is flagged like any other, and its note says why the percentage on that day means little.

A morning call that didn't arrive is not a flag. It's reported in the day's note. A low published probability that didn't come in isn't a failure of anything; it's the forecast saying what it said.

Every flag is listed on the week summary with its state:

  • open: reviewed in next week's report;
  • closed: with the finding, and the date it was investigated and approved.

A flag closes only on an approved finding. We don't print an explanation we haven't investigated, and we don't close a flag by letting it age out.

The day strip

Each day has two bars, the plan beside what actually happened. The left bar is the share of that day's own ceiling the plan captured (the day table's PLAN CAPTURE, % OF CEILING). The right bar, hatched so a black-and-white print tells the two apart, is the share of the same ceiling you captured: your own settled dispatch, the dollars under RECORDED & PLAN DEVIATION. It's teal where the recorded share is within 10% of the plan's share that day or above it, and rust only where it is more than 10% below it, the same line the day table prints in red (from 9 October 2026; a day a little off the plan is not shown as missing it, since small reasons can move a day a little). Each bar's percentage is printed beneath it, the plan's first. Bars above and below the rule share one scale, so a small loss is a short bar. The rule is zero. A left bar below it is a day the plan finished behind, where what the spread paid didn't cover what the round trip cost, and that day carries the sentence in its NOTES cell. A right bar below it is a day your own dispatch lost money. The caption under the strip reads Plan · recorded · % of each day's ceiling, followed by the colour rule. On a preliminary weekly only the plan's bars are drawn, and the caption says what you captured is awaiting settlement.

Micro-opportunity days

Some days have almost nothing in them. Where the whole day's ceiling is worth less than two full cycles of your battery's wear allowance, a capture percentage is a large ratio of very little, and it moves the week's total by almost nothing. Those days say so in their note, and the dollars beside the percentage are the figure to read. The note changes nothing else; the flag rule still applies to the day's capture.

Where revenue was left

Under the day table, Where revenue was left lists the days the as-run capture fell more than 10% short of the plan captured that day. That's the day table's own line: a day listed here is a day whose percentage prints in red. At most three days are listed, the largest gap in dollars first, and the figure beside the heading is the total of the listed days' gaps. On a day that carried ancillary service the plan and the as-run capture are the figures measured on the capacity traded, so an hour in reserve never counts here, and a paused day is never listed.

Each row draws the day on a 24-hour strip, the same one the dashboard draws for today. The plan's charge and discharge blocks are solid inside the track, read from the plan as issued. The settled dispatch, as run, is a lighter, outlined band standing a little above and below the track, in the colour of what the system did: discharge in the plan's discharge colour, charge in its charge colour. Where a band sits over a block of its own colour, the system followed the plan. A band over an empty track is dispatch the plan didn't ask for, and a block with no band over it is hours left. The strip runs midnight to midnight by the clock, so the hours of the dispatch day that fall after midnight sit at its left, and a faint line rises from the clock faces at every hour, darker at 06, 12 and 18. A key under the table names the four marks. Hours with the whole battery in reserve are hatched over the track, under the plan's blocks, and the key then names a fifth, in reserve; hours divided between reserve and trade are traded hours and draw nothing. Then the plan captured, the as-run capture, and the gap: first as a percentage of the plan captured, then in dollars, so every dollar figure sits in the last column. A day with no dispatch shows 100%. A percentage above 100% means the dispatch lost money that day.

The strip also carries the energy, in MWh, as numbers alone; the captions at its left name the rows. Above each band, Recorded MWh is the energy the system moved in that block, the sum of its settled intervals. Under each plan block, in bold orange, Schedule MWh is the energy the plan scheduled in that block. Both are measured at the grid: a charge figure is energy drawn from the grid, a discharge figure is energy delivered to it. Across a full cycle, the difference between what was drawn and what was delivered is the battery's own losses at the round-trip efficiency on the order form, which the plan is built with, split evenly between charging and discharging. Where two numbers would overlap, the first keeps its place and the second drops one line down.

Under its strip, a row can carry three notes, and only these three:

  • inside a $300–$499 spike: the gap sat inside a spike, named by that spike's own band. A row without it had no gap inside a spike.
  • below the baseline: the as-run capture that day came in under the baseline for the day.
  • measured on the 98 MW traded: the day carried ancillary service, and its figures are measured on the capacity traded.

A week with no such day has no Where revenue was left section. No note in the day table names the worst day; this section ranks the days instead.

Baseline, Measured Gain, and the fee

The three ledgers

what it is
A — your baselinewhat your battery would have earned on its own, fixed at sign-up and recorded on your executed order form
B — recordedwhat you actually banked, from your own settlement data
C — the planwhat full adherence to the plans we issued would have earned, on the same days and the same battery

All three are computed by the same code on the same settled prices, and all three are net of the wear allowance, applied identically to each.

How the fee is worked out

Your baseline is what your battery would have earned on its own, fixed at sign-up. Our plan is what full adherence would have earned. You pay a share of the gain over your baseline, measured week by week on whichever gain is smaller that week: the one your own dispatch made (B − A) or the one the plan was worth (C − A). Beat the plan in a week and the excess is yours, uncharged. Fall short and you pay only on what you captured.

The smaller of the two, taken week by week and added up over the quarter, is the Measured Gain. That's the fee base.

Retained is your gain over baseline (B − A) less the Neurevo fee: what the quarter left with you.

Each week's fee part is your rate on the smaller of that week's two gains. Your rate is on your order form; standard terms are 20%. On a week your own dispatch beat the plan, the report says so beside the figure: what you made beyond the plan is yours to keep and none of it bears a fee.

A day with a late issue isn't billed. An issue is on time if it reaches your shelf within one hour of its time: the 06:00 issue by 07:00 CT, the 13:45 issue by 14:45 CT. Where either of a day's issues is later than that, or never arrives, the whole day is out of your fee. Nothing of that day is billed, and its gain over your baseline is yours. A day whose plan was re-issued after the fact is never billed either. The day stays in your table, marked (for example filed late · day not billed · 06:00 issue filed 12:36 CT), and its plan still counts for capture.

The initiation advance is shown only while it's being credited. Each weekly and Quarterly Report in whose run part of it is credited against your fees says how much has been applied and how much remains, and the one in whose run it's used up says so. A balance left at a Quarterly Report carries into the next quarter's first fees. Once it's applied in full, it isn't mentioned again.

The netting terms

A week below your baseline shows a fee of $0.00. Its shortfall reduces the quarter's fee base, and if the quarter ends below your baseline the shortfall carries forward into the next one. Under standard terms the netting is done on the quarter, with a zero floor and no lock-in: a quarter whose Measured Gain comes out at or below zero is billed nothing, and there is never a negative invoice. On your weekly's week-by-week table and on the Quarterly Report, the fee column adds up to the quarter's fee through a netting row above the total. Each week below your baseline keeps its $0.00, and the netting row carries their shortfall.

The week-by-week table

Each row is one week. Its columns read, in order: WEEK, DAYS, THE PLAN, UNDER PLAN, ABOVE PLAN, RECORDED, BASELINE, MEASURED GAIN, and NEUREVO FEE. The plan, under plan, above plan, and the baseline are set in grey, for reference. The fee is worked out in the columns set in full ink: recorded, the measured gain, and the Neurevo fee. On a week the battery carried ancillary service, the plan and the figures under and above it are measured on the capacity traded, as the fee is, so a paused day adds nothing to them (from 10 October 2026). The dated correction line at the top of a restated weekly still compares the plan before the ancillary envelope, so the figure it restates moved for the rule it names alone.

Baseline is what the week's measurable days would have earned on your own pre-Neurevo pattern, ledger A one week at a time. The plan and the recorded figure are both measured against it. The quarter-to-date row adds up the column, and the total is the figure the report prints as "Baseline on the measurable days (A)".

Under plan is what the week's take fell short of the plan by, shown in parentheses the way accounts write a shortfall: ($6,822.21). Above plan is what the week beat the plan by, shown plainly. A week fills one of the two; a week that ran the plan exactly shows $0.00 under plan. The quarter-to-date row adds up each column on its own, so a week above the plan is never netted away against the weeks below it, and what a week made above the plan is never charged.

Measured gain is the plan's gain over your baseline, or your own gain if that was less: the figure the week's fee is charged on. The quarter-to-date row adds up the column, and the total is the figure the fee block prints as "Measured Gain — the lesser of the two, week by week, and the fee base". A week below your baseline shows its measured gain below zero and a Neurevo fee of $0.00, with the netting stated under the fee. The netting row above the quarter-to-date total carries it, so the fee column adds up to the total beneath it. A quarter that ends below your baseline shows the same $0.00 in the quarter-to-date row as in the fee block, with the shortfall carried forward beside it.

The DAYS column counts the days each figure is taken over and names the ones it leaves out. A day the billing rule put out of the fee (plan re-issued after the fact, filed late, or not filed) is counted as not billed: its plan was measured and still counts for capture in the day table, but no fee is taken on it. A day outside the measurement altogether (before your service began, no plan of record from us, not graded yet, or not yet happened) is counted as not measured. The note under the table names every such day and its reason. "Measurable days", in the fee block, are the days the fee is taken on.

The table lists settled weeks only. Days of the quarter still waiting on your settlement data aren't reported yet; one line under the table counts them (N days of this quarter are awaiting settlement and are not yet reported) and gives no figure for them. A week that wasn't measured shows a dash in every money column.

Your gains as a percentage of your baseline

In the fee block, under each of the two gains over your baseline, one line states that gain as a percentage of your baseline, in bold. It's the gain on the line above divided by your baseline on the measurable days (A), both as printed, to two decimal places, so you can check it with a calculator.

  • Under gain over baseline (B − A): what your own dispatch earned above your baseline, as a percentage of it.
  • Under the plans' gain over baseline (C − A): what full adherence to the plans was worth above your baseline, as a percentage of it.

Both cover the measurable days only, the same days as the three ledgers. While the fee ledger isn't yet measurable, no percentage is shown.

Two bases, one plan

Where you've committed capacity to ancillary services, that capacity couldn't also be sold into energy. The plan is therefore measured twice: before the ancillary capacity envelope, and after it, with the envelope applied interval by interval, which is line (C) of the fee ledger. The fee is computed on the after figure. The day table prints the plan before the envelope except on a day that carried ancillary service and isn't paused, where it's measured on the capacity traded; the week total row, the figures at the top of the report and the week-by-week table are measured on the capacity traded, as line (C) is (from 10 October 2026; until then this sentence said the day table always printed the plan before the envelope).

Both are printed so the difference is checkable. Where no capacity was committed, the two are the same figure and the weekly doesn't print the reconciliation. On a day that carried ancillary service and isn't paused the day table, too, is measured on the capacity you traded; see Days your battery carried ancillary service.

What is not in these figures

Simulated dispatch, not profit and loss. Tolling terms, your own degradation and O&M accounting, and your ancillary-services obligations are yours and aren't modelled. The one exception is the stated wear allowance, which is a term of the measurement, not a figure from your costs.

Where the weekly's numbers come from

The fingerprint

Every weekly prints the first twelve characters of a SHA-256 over the graded rows it was built from, with the window those rows cover beside it. The full digest is on the verification surface in your portal, where it can be checked against the same rows. The prefix keeps the row readable; the window makes the digest recomputable.

Plan of record

Every day is measured against the plan exactly as it was archived when it was issued to you, never re-planned with hindsight. The provenance row says where each day's plan was read from.

When a grade can still move

Two clocks appear on the weekly and they aren't the same clock. The plan is frozen at issue and never re-run; the prices are as ERCOT has settled them.

A graded window is frozen at first settlement and doesn't move again. Where ERCOT later resettles a day inside a frozen window, we issue a dated correction to the report rather than changing it in place, and the correction reaches your shelf as the revision in force. A week graded before 31 August 2026 follows ERCOT's own resettlements instead, and its report says so, because the answer is a fact about that week's dates.

The price basis

Every dollar on your report is settled at your own hub, and your plan is built on that hub's prices. Where a day's plan was built on another hub's prices, the report says so on page one, and the difference between the two hubs is already inside that day's figures. It isn't added or removed afterwards.

The wear allowance

A fixed allowance per MWh discharged, netted from all three ledgers identically. It's a term of the measurement, agreed in advance, and the one cost of yours that appears in these figures.

Ancillary capacity

Every measurable day carries a record of what capacity you committed to ancillary services. Where that record shows zero across the measurement window, the provenance row says so and states the window it verified over, the same window the report bills on.

Plans filed to the archive

The weekly carries a seven-day strip showing which days' morning plans are filed in your archive. It reports that the plan is on your shelf, which is a different claim from the time it reached you. Where a filing is missing, the report names the days and says plainly that the plans were issued and are scored; what's missing is the copy on your shelf, and that's ours to fix.

Report numbering

Weeklies are numbered sequentially from your first issued report. No. 001 is the first weekly you were sent.

The quarter close

Each quarter closes on a document of its own, the Quarterly Report: one per quarter, numbered on its own sequence and filed on your Reports shelf with its own tag. It issues once the quarter's last day has settled on your settlement route (about ten days after the quarter ends through ERCOT's report stream, about sixty through ERCOT's public disclosure) and it is final on issue.

Page one opens with the quarter's three figures, in this order: recorded (your settled dispatch on the quarter's measurable days, net of the wear allowance and before our fee); gain over baseline, with the plans' gain over it alongside; and the Neurevo fee, with the Measured Gain it's a share of. Each is a line of the quarter's ledger, printed again. The invoice line follows them, under the Quarterly Report's own number: Quarterly Report No. 001 is billed as Invoice No. 001. When the initiation advance pays some or all of the fee, the invoice line says how much it took.

After them come, in order: the fee paragraph; the quarter's ledger (baseline A, recorded B, the plan C, the two gains, and the Measured Gain); what carried in from the previous quarter, what was netted this quarter, and what carries out to the next, with zeros printed where there's nothing; the initiation advance, when part of it was credited in the quarter; the week-by-week table, whose fee column adds up to the quarter's fee; the finality line; and where every figure came from, over the quarter's own days. The days, the spikes, and the flags stay in your weeklies.

The weekly report whose week contains the quarter's last day (the first weekly issued after it) doesn't carry the close. It carries one pointer line under its quarter to date: which of its days closed on the Quarterly Report, that report's number, and how many measurable days the new quarter opens with. The Quarterly Report is the document the quarter is billed on.

A day belongs to the calendar quarter its date falls in, so a quarter normally begins and ends with a part-week. When the quarter's last day isn't a Sunday, that week runs on into the new quarter: its days up to and including the quarter's last day are billed in the closed quarter and stated on its Quarterly Report, and the weekly's quarter to date opens with the rest. For the fee, each day is billed in the quarter it belongs to, so such a week is two part-weeks under the week-by-week rule, one in each quarter, and the weekly's fee on the week shows the part billed in each. The new quarter's figures start from zero. Anything the closed quarter carries forward (a shortfall, or the part of the initiation advance already applied) is taken from the quarter's own closing figures, the same figures its Quarterly Report prints.

Resettlement after a Quarterly Report issues

A Quarterly Report is final on issue. If ERCOT later resettles a day inside it, the next Quarterly Report to issue carries a dated true-up line for that quarter: the days (or the week) resettled, the fee as filed, the fee as resettled, and the difference. It carries the difference, less any part of the initiation advance it takes up, into its own invoice, with anything that quarter carries forward taken from its resettled figures. The issued report doesn't change.

What changed

A document on this shelf sometimes carries a note near the top: "Corrected", with a short reason and a date. This chapter explains what that means.

What a re-issue is

Every brief, mid-day update, and report you receive is kept. When something about how one is presented needs fixing (never a figure, a time, or the plan itself), we don't edit the document quietly. We issue it again, in full, and keep the old one reachable alongside it.

The document as first sent is always available as revision 0. Each later revision carries its own dated reason. The newest revision is the one in force, the one you should read, but every earlier one stays on file in order, so nothing that was said is erased or rewritten after the fact.

When a re-issue is sent: a re-issue that changes only how a document says something waits, and the week's corrections of that kind go out together with your weekly report on Monday, under one note. A correction that moves a figure (a fee, a total, a date you rely on) is never held. It's sent the moment it's made, with its own note saying what moved.

What never changes in a re-issue

A re-issue never moves a spike probability, a time, an hour window, or a dispatch instruction. What the plan said stays what the plan said. A re-issue may correct a derived figure (a fee, a quarter-to-date total, a report number) only when a rule we apply to every client has changed, and then the correction note on the shelf says which rule and what it did. On a weekly report that's a dated line under the masthead with the figures before and after, and the earlier revision stays on file beside it. If a dollar amount differs between two revisions and the note doesn't say why, that's a defect; tell us. Everything else a re-issue corrects is how something is said, never what the day's plan reported.
